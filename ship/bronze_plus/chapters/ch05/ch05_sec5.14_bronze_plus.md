#### 5.14 Consensus Truthiness (The Death of Safety)
The final vector of the "Accelerant" was the weaponization of the Crowd itself. The precedent was Gamergate (2014). It is often dismissed as a "culture war" over video games, but epistemologically, it was the beta test for the Heckler's Veto. It proved that if you generate enough Volume (threats, doxing, swatting), you can silence the Expert. The platform architecture—specifically the Quote Tweet (introduced 2015)—was designed to facilitate this. It allowed the mob to broadcast a target to millions instantly, creating a "Notification Storm" that rendered the victim's device unusable. The economics were asymmetrical. It cost the "Swarm" $0 to send a death threat. It cost the victim thousands of dollars to secure their home, scrub their data, and hire lawyers.

The mitigation from the users was Algospeak. To survive the hostile architecture and the automated moderation that followed, users broke their own language. They stopped saying "Suicide" and started saying "Unalive". They replaced "Sex" with "Seggs" and "Porn" with "Corn" [Lorenz, Taylor (2022), "Internet 'algospeak' is changing our language in real time, from 'nip nops' to 'le dollar bean'", The Washington Post, URL: https://www.washingtonpost.com/technology/2022/04/08/algospeak-tiktok-le-dollar-bean/]. We didn't make the internet safer; we just made the language dumber. The result was the "Sensible Exodus". The experts—journalists, academics, women, minorities—realized the "Town Square" was a kill box. Data from Amnesty International [Amnesty International (2018), "#ToxicTwitter: Violence and Abuse Against Women Online", Amnesty International Research Report, URL: https://www.amnesty.org/en/latest/research/2018/03/toxic-twitter-a-toxic-place-for-women/]. and Nature [Nature News Staff (2023), "Thousands of scientists are cutting back on Twitter, seeding angst and uncertainty", Nature, 620, pp. 482–484, DOI: 10.1038/d41586-023-02554-0, URL: https://doi.org/10.1038/d41586-023-02554-0] confirms the shift: nearly a third of women who faced harassment ceased posting their opinions, while over 50% of surveyed scientists reduced their engagement or abandoned the platform entirely.

They didn't just disappear; they moved. Following the dynamic described by Yancey Strickler, they retreated to the "Dark Forest" of the internet—private Discords, Slack channels, and newsletters [Strickler, Yancey (2019), "The Dark Forest Theory of the Internet", OneZero, URL: https://onezero.medium.com/the-dark-forest-theory-of-the-internet-7dc3e68a7cb1]. They left the public feed to the Trolls, the Bots, and the Zombies. The "Open Web" didn't die because the servers crashed; it died because the people who knew things left the room. And this was the dataset—poisoned, performative, and abandoned—that we were about to feed to the AI.
