#### 5.13 Performative Truthiness (The Death of Genre)
While state actors used the algorithm for subversion, violent non-state actors used it for Spectacle. They realized that the "Open Web" had erased the distinction between War, Crime, and Entertainment. ISIS was the pioneer. By 2015, they had rejected the grainy aesthetic of Al-Qaeda in favor of 4K, multi-camera productions that mimicked Hollywood trailers. They didn't need a news network; they had the feed. They weaponized the Production Value of the West against it [Berger, J. M., & Morgan, Jonathon (2015), "The ISIS Twitter Census: Defining and describing the population of ISIS supporters on Twitter", The Brookings Project on U.S. Relations with the Islamic World, Analysis Paper No. 20, The Brookings Institution, URL: https://www.brookings.edu/research/the-isis-twitter-census-defining-and-describing-the-population-of-isis-supporters-on-twitter/].

Then came the Gamification of Terror. In 2019, the Christchurch shooter didn't just commit mass murder; he Livestreamed it. He mounted a GoPro to his helmet, explicitly mimicking the "First-Person Shooter" (FPS) perspective of video games. He designed the violence to be meme-able, shouting "Subscribe to PewDiePie" before opening fire. He wasn't speaking to a political ideology; he was speaking to Chan Culture [Macklin, Graham (2019), "The Christchurch Attacks: Livestream Terror in the Viral Video Age", CTC Sentinel, Combating Terrorism Center at West Point, 12(6), URL: https://ctc.westpoint.edu/the-christchurch-attacks-livestream-terror-in-the-viral-video-age/]. The "Knowledge System" failed because it treated the livestream as "Content"—technically indistinguishable from a gaming stream—until the bodies fell.

This was the only area where the institutions found an effective mitigation: The Hash Database. The major platforms formed the Global Internet Forum to Counter Terrorism (GIFCT) to share "hashes" (digital fingerprints) of terrorist videos. They successfully automated the removal of ISIS beheadings and the Christchurch video. They could ban the File, but they couldn't ban the Genre. The adaptation was immediate. Mexican Cartels realized they couldn't post gore (it gets hashed), so they pivoted to "Lifestyle Content". On #CartelTok, they posted "Narco-Bling"—gold-plated AK-47s, pet tigers, and stacks of cash set to narcocorrido ballads. They normalized the aesthetic of mass murder by wrapping it in the visual language of an Influencer Flex (Fox News, 2025). The epistemology of violence had shifted. It was no longer something to be reported on; it was something to be produced for.
