#### 5.15 The Last Vestige of Web 2.0 (The Ideology of the Feed)
"If you want to free a society, you just need to give them internet access. If you want to control a society, you don't need to censor them; you just need to flood the zone with noise."
— After Sean Parker

To understand the magnitude of the loss, we must first strip away the corporate branding. Twitter was never a "Social Network" in the way Facebook was. It was a utility. It was the last surviving artifact of the Web 2.0 ideology, built on the same "Dispatch" protocols that powered RSS (Really Simple Syndication). Jack Dorsey didn’t build it to connect you with your high school friends; he built it to answer the question, "What is happening right now?" [Dorsey, Jack (2011), "Jack Dorsey on the Origins of Twitter and Real-Time Information Flow" [Video], TechCrunch / YouTube, URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=H8HqQ3z8uL8]. The architecture reflected this.

For the first decade, the feed was strictly Chronological. You subscribed to a signal, and you received that signal, unfiltered, in the order it occurred. Critics like Eli Pariser called these "Filter Bubbles" and treated them as a danger to democracy [Pariser, Eli (2011), The Filter Bubble: What the Internet Is Hiding from You, Penguin Press, ISBN: 978-1-59420-300-8, URL: https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/309444/the-filter-bubble-by-eli-pariser/]. They were wrong. The chronological bubble was not a prison; it was a Trust Graph. Because the user was the Editor, a user could construct a dashboard of 500 epidemiologists and virologists, creating a P4-Lab-level view of a pandemic that outperformed the CDC. A copyright lawyer could curate a feed of 200 peers and see a legal precedent evolve in real-time. This was User Sovereignty. We didn't need the algorithm to tell us what was important. We knew what was important because we had chosen who to trust. I knew about the airstrike in Gaza or the bank run in Silicon Valley hours before the New York Times push notification, because I was watching the raw feed of the experts the Times reporters were scrambling to interview.

The platform hated this. The "Optimization Team" saw the chronological feed as inefficient because it didn't maximize "Time on Site". In 2016, they tried to kill it, introducing the algorithmic "Home" timeline to mimic Facebook. The users revolted. They didn't want "Engagement"; they wanted "Now". The backlash was so severe that in December 2018, Twitter capitulated, introducing the "Sparkle Button"—a toggle that allowed users to revert to the chronological stream [Twitter Product Team (2018), "Bringing you the latest Tweets: A chronological home timeline", Twitter Blog, URL: https://blog.twitter.com/en_us/topics/product/2018/bringing-you-the-latest-tweets]. It was the last time the Users won a decisive victory against the Machine.
