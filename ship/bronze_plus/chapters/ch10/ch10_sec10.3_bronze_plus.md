### 10.3 The Hostile Takeover: The "Race to the Top"

The takeover began not with a philosophy, but with a check.

In 2009, the Obama Administration, led by Secretary of Education **Arne Duncan**, launched a program with a name that sounded like a game show and a mechanism that functioned like a bribe: **"Race to the Top."**

The logic was pure neoliberal coercion. The federal government dangled a pot of **$4.35 billion** in front of cash-starved state governments, which were reeling from the 2008 financial collapse. To qualify for a slice of the money, states had to agree to adopt a set of "Common Standards." The brilliance of the mechanism was its voluntary nature. No federal law mandated the Common Core; the states "chose" it, driven by the desperation of their own balance sheets [U.S. Department of Education (2009), "Race to the Top Program: Executive Summary", U.S. Department of Education, URL: https://www2.ed.gov/programs/racetothetop/executive-summary.pdf].

But who wrote the standards?

They were not written by university presidents, nor by a congress of veteran teachers, nor by the parents of the "Dropout Factories." They were written in a private room by a private organization—**Student Achievement Partners**—funded largely by the **Bill and Melinda Gates Foundation**. The Foundation poured over **$200 million** into the development and promotion of the Common Core, effectively purchasing the curriculum of the entire nation [Lyndsey Layton (2014), "How Bill Gates pulled off the swift Common Core revolution", The Washington Post, URL: https://www.washingtonpost.com/politics/how-bill-gates-pulled-off-the-swift-common-core-revolution/2014/06/07/a830e32e-ec34-11e3-9f5c-9075d5508f0a_story.html].

The architects of this new order—**David Coleman**, **Jason Zimba**, and **Susan Pimentel**—were not educators. They were consultants. They viewed the chaotic landscape of American education through the lens of **Industrial Engineering**. They did not see the "Zip Code Apartheid" as a moral failure requiring resource redistribution; they saw it as a "Variance Problem" requiring standardization.

Their solution was not to lift the floor, but to flatten the entire structure. They reasoned that if they could not equalize the *inputs* (money, safe neighborhoods, stable homes), they would brutally standardize the *outputs*. They would design a "Universal Tool"—a student who could process information in a uniform way, regardless of their location, background, or soul.
