### 1.12 Just Make It Plausible
Sometimes the LLM gets it right; sometimes it makes stuff up. So what do we do about it? The industry apparently decided that since it is hard to fix the machine, they must fix the user. They faced a binary choice: fix the architecture or fix the user’s expectations. They chose the user.

In February 2026, Will Douglas Heaven, the senior AI editor at MIT Technology Review, published a definitive analysis of the industry's stance [Heaven, Will Douglas (2026), "What We've Been Getting Wrong About AI's Truth Crisis", MIT Technology Review, URL: https://www.technologyreview.com/2026/01/15/ai-truth-crisis-hallucinations/]. In a piece titled "What We've Been Getting Wrong About AI's Truth Crisis," Heaven—a computer scientist (PhD, Imperial College) and founding editor of the award-winning BBC Future Now—argued that the public obsession with "hallucinations" was a category error.

Citing the foundational work of OpenAI researcher Andrej Karpathy [Karpathy, Andrej (2023), "State of GPT: Insights into LLM Training and Inference Dynamics", Microsoft Build 2023 / Keynote Transcripts, URL: https://build.microsoft.com/en-US/sessions/db3f4859-da3e-4a5f-b419-e44f1e71a61d], Heaven posited that the machine was never designed to act as a database of truth. As Karpathy famously described the architecture:

"I always like to say that LLMs are dream machines. We direct their dreams with prompts. The hallucinations are the feature, not the bug... We are asking a dream machine to be a search engine."

The error, Heaven argued, lay not in the machine’s tendency to confabulate, but in the user’s insistence that it shouldn't.

This moribund shift in the epistemological contract has civilization-level implications. For forty years, the goal of the Information Age was to reduce uncertainty. When Tim Berners-Lee designed the original protocol at CERN [Berners-Lee, Tim (1989), "Information Management: A Proposal", CERN European Organization for Nuclear Research, CERN-ICS-1989-001, URL: https://www.w3.org/History/1989/proposal.html], his specific mission was to create a "shared information space" where scientists could link to verifiable facts, creating a system where "a person could be sure that the document they were reading was the one that was written."

Heaven’s "Confession of Plausibility" reverses this. He suggests that we should stop viewing the AI as a reference tool and start viewing it as a "reasoning engine," where plausibility—not accuracy—is the metric of success. If the output sounds right, it is useful.

This defense is the rejection of not just logic but even the idea of logic. It admits that the industry has built a machine that cannot distinguish between a verifiable ground truth and a statistically probable fiction. 
It asks us to accept a "reasoning engine" that has been decoupled from the truth. It is a trap that the Stoic philosopher Epictetus identified two thousand years ago [Epictetus (c. 108 AD), Discourses, Book II, Chapter 25 ("How is logic necessary?"), translated by Robin Hard, Oxford World's Classics (2014), ISBN: 978-0-19-959518-1, URL: https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0236%3Atext%3Ddisc%3Abook%3D2%3Achapter%3D25]. In his Discourses, Epictetus describes a student who walked into his lecture hall and demanded: "Convince me that logic is necessary."

Epictetus did not offer a long-winded lecture. He looked at the man and asked: "Do you wish me to demonstrate this to you?"

"Yes," the student replied.

"Then," said Epictetus, "I must use a demonstrative form of argument." The student agreed. "But how then," Epictetus asked, "will you know whether I am cheating you with a fallacy?"

The student fell silent. He realized the trap: to even ask for a proof that logic is necessary, he had to first use logic to evaluate the proof. He needed a scale to tell if Epictetus was lying to him. Without the scale, the demonstration was just noise.

By selling us a "reasoning engine" that rejects the rules of truth, the industry is trying to sell us a scale that has no markings. If a machine cannot distinguish between a verifiable fact and a statistically probable fiction, it is not "reasoning", it is merely performing a high-speed pantomime of intelligence. Aristotle’s verdict on those who reject the law of non-contradiction, the idea that a thing cannot be both true and false at the same time, remains the final word [Aristotle (c. 350 BC), Metaphysics, Book IV (Gamma), 1006a, translated by W. D. Ross, Oxford University Press (1924), URL: https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0052%3Abook%3D4%3Asection%3D1006a]. He argued that a person who refuses to acknowledge the distinction between a fact and a fiction has surrendered the ability to speak. They are no longer a participant in human civilization. They are, in his words, "no better than a vegetable."

Rather than halting deployment to restore the truth benchmark, the technorati advises the public to accept the guesstimate as the new standard of knowledge.
