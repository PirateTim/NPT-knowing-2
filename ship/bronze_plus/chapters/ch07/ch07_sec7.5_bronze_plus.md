### 7.5 The End of Ownership: The Ubisoft Precedent

If the Haworth acquisition proved they could liquidate the *publisher*, the case of *The Crew* proved they could liquidate the *product* in your hand.

In March 2024, the video game giant **Ubisoft** shut down the servers for *The Crew*, a racing game played by 12 million people [Ubisoft Customer Support (2024), "Ubisoft Statement on The Crew Server Decommission and License Access", Ubisoft Connect Announcement, URL: https://www.ubisoft.com/en-us/help/purchases-and-rewards/article/closure-of-online-services-for-the-crew/000106886]. But they didn't just turn off the multiplayer mode. Because the game required a constant "handshake" with the server to verify the license, the shutdown rendered the physical discs and digital downloads completely inert.

Customers who had paid $60 for the game found that they owned nothing. They had not purchased a game; they had purchased a **"Revocable Access License."**

**Ross Scott**, a YouTuber who launched the "Stop Killing Games" campaign, identified this as a crossing of the Rubicon. "This is not about video games," Scott testified. "This is about the destruction of property rights." [Scott, R. (2024), "Stop Killing Games: Consumer Protection Campaign on Digital Goods and Software Preservation", Accursed Farms / Stop Killing Games, URL: https://www.stopkillinggames.com/].

He is right. The logic applied to *The Crew* is the same logic applied to the digital library. In the "Dependency Model," you never own the book, the game, or the record. You are merely renting a window into the server. And as soon as the cost of electricity exceeds the value of your subscription, the landlord will brick the window.

The "Gentleman’s Agreement" of 1998 assumed that if you bought a copy, you owned it. The reality of 2026 is that you are only allowed to see it as long as the machine finds it profitable to show you.
