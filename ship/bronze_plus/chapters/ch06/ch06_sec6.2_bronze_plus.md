### 6.2 Founders and the Law
There where it is we do not need the wall:
He is all pine and I am apple orchard.
My apple trees will never get across
And eat the cones under his pines, I tell him.
He only says, ‘Good fences make good neighbors.’
— Robert Frost, *Mending Wall* (1914)

The speaker in Frost’s narrative serves as the prototype for intellectual negligence. He views the labor of maintaining the wall as an "outdoor game," a tedious inconvenience born of a neighbor’s "old-stone savage" superstition. To the speaker, the stones are self-evident and self-sustaining; he believes that because his "apple trees will never get across" to eat pine cones, the structure separating the two is a redundant vanity. He represents the **Statistical Decoupling** mindset: the belief that facts can exist in a vacuum, separated from the labor of their arrangement. The neighbor, conversely, understands that the wall exists to define the identity of the orchard itself.

In the American knowledge system, this neighbor is represented by the unanimous consensus of September 5, 1787. The Intellectual Property clause of the Constitution (Art. I, § 8, cl. 8) emerged from a summer defined by the friction of compromise, yet the power to "promote the Progress of Science and useful Arts" was adopted without a single dissenting vote. The Framers viewed the "exclusive Right" as a mandatory evidentiary standard. James Madison’s defense in *Federalist No. 43* (1788) [James Madison (1788), "Federalist No. 43: The Same Subject Continued: The Powers Conferred by the Constitution Further Considered", New York Packet, URL: https://avalon.law.yale.edu/18th_century/fed43.asp] makes this explicit: the "public good fully coincides" with the claims of individuals because the alternative is the dissolution of authority.
