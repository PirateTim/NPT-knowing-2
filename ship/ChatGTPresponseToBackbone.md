They’re outlining a **book-length essay/argument about the collapse of society’s ability to distinguish knowledge from plausible-sounding information**, with **AI as a major accelerant but not the sole cause**.

The central thesis appears to be:

> **Modern institutions are systematically destroying the provenance, context, verification, and human custodianship that allow people to know whether something is true—and generative AI then industrializes that failure.**

The document explicitly frames the end state as **“negative knowledge”**: not simply *not knowing*, or even having misinformation, but **confidently believing things that have no reliable connection to reality**. 

### The argument's progression

The chapters trace that problem through increasingly large systems:

1. **Introduction — The End of Knowing**
   Establishes the conceptual framework: knowledge requires provenance, context, authorship, and verification. Digital systems have progressively severed those connections. 

2. **Truthiness and public discourse**
   The essay argues that American political rhetoric, education, media, and technology increasingly privilege things that *sound* true over things that can be demonstrated to be true. 

3. **LLMs**
   It then turns to AI as the technological embodiment of this problem: models trained on enormous quantities of text without reliable provenance, producing plausible language rather than necessarily grounded knowledge. 

4. **The economics of data**
   The next argument is that AI companies supposedly treat provenance as economically worthless—preferring enormous quantities of scraped material over carefully curated, verified knowledge. 

5. **Journalism**
   Journalism becomes another example: the author argues that reporting has shifted from independently establishing facts toward transmitting statements from authoritative sources, creating a self-reinforcing information loop. 

6. **The open web and platforms**
   The essay then expands from *creation* of information to its **preservation**. Platform consolidation, algorithmic incentives, SEO spam, account monetization, and disappearing websites supposedly transform the web from a human archive into transient commercial infrastructure. 

7. **Law and intellectual property**
   Copyright and fair-use law are presented as another institutional mechanism that can separate information from its creator and provenance. 

8. **Digital impermanence**
   The argument gets broader still: things that people once *owned* or that institutions once *archived* increasingly become revocable digital services. The author calls the resulting destruction of historical records a **“Digital Bamiyan.”** 

9. **Scientific publishing**
   Peer review, citation metrics, paper mills, retractions, and AI training are connected into another version of the same problem: fraudulent information can enter the scholarly record and subsequently become part of computational systems. 

10. **Education**
    The essay argues that education itself has been stripping away some of the intellectual tools—context, formal logic, warrants, historical provenance—that enable students to interrogate claims rather than merely process them. 

11. **Political/cognitive warfare**
    Finally, social media, state actors, troll farms, propaganda, and engagement algorithms are presented as mechanisms for deliberately overwhelming the public's ability to distinguish genuine events from manufactured narratives. 

12. **The state loses its own witnesses**
    The final chapter argues that governments become especially vulnerable when they eliminate the civil servants, scientists, meteorologists, archivists, and other people who actually possess institutional memory and firsthand knowledge. The endpoint is metaphorically **“Flying by Vibes”**—a government making decisions after destroying its own capacity to perceive reality. 

### So, in one sentence

**They're writing an indictment of the modern information ecosystem, arguing that the destruction of provenance and verification—from journalism and education to scientific publishing, digital archives, government, and ultimately AI—could leave civilization with an unprecedented condition where people have more information but progressively less actual knowledge.**

One particularly interesting feature is that **the essay isn't really “about AI.”** AI is portrayed as the *culmination and industrial-scale amplifier* of a much older epistemological decay. The recurring three-part progression is essentially:

**human/institutional decay → technology scales it → institutions stop taking responsibility for verification.** 

And the writer has given that thesis a very dramatic name: ***The End of Knowing*.**
