#### 5.11 Visual Truthiness (The Death of Time)
"The algorithm does not want you to be happy. It wants you to be predictable."
— Jaron Lanier (2018)

"If you want to keep a secret, you must also hide it from yourself."
— George Orwell, 1984

In 2016, the social web made a quiet, fatal adjustment to its architecture. For a decade, the "Feed" had been reverse-chronological. If a user posted a photo at 9:00 AM, you saw it after the photo posted at 8:59 AM. Truth was anchored in Time. Then, Twitter and Instagram killed the chronology. They replaced the "Timeline" with the "Algorithmic Feed". The rationale was "Relevance"—the platform would guess what you wanted to see and show it to you first. The engineers believed they were optimizing for engagement. In reality, they were destroying the metadata of verification. The algorithm didn't care when something happened; it only cared how it made you feel.

This birthed the era of Visual Truthiness. The most potent weapon of this era was the "Zombie Video". Consider the case of the McKinney Pool Party. In June 2015, a video circulated showing Cpl. Eric Casebolt of the McKinney, Texas police department pinning a 15-year-old Black girl in a bikini to the ground. It was a horrific, verified document of police brutality. Casebolt resigned; the event was news (WFAA, 2015). But in June 2020, during the height of the George Floyd protests, the video resurfaced. It flooded Twitter and Facebook feeds, shared by millions of outraged users who believed they were witnessing a new atrocity. The algorithm, seeing the keywords "Police" and "Pool" and detecting the high engagement (rage), stripped the 2015 timestamp and served it as "Breaking News". The video was "True" (the event happened), but the experience was a Lie (Context Collapse). The user was experiencing 2015 trauma as 2020 reality. The "Open Web" had become a machine for Computational Truthiness—it delivered content that felt true to the moment, regardless of its relationship to the timeline.

The mitigation for this was the Fact Check, a tool that proved to be structurally impotent. Facebook and Twitter added "Missing Context" labels, but these corrections moved at human speed—days or weeks—while the Zombie Video moved at algorithmic speed. By the time the "Truth" arrived, the emotional imprint of the "Lie" was already calcified in the neural pathways of the electorate. We had built a system where the Correction was always a eulogy for the Truth.
