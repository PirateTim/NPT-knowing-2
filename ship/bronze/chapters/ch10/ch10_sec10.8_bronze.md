### 10.8 The Evidence Ignored: The Baseball Study

The architects also ignored the forensic evidence regarding *how* human beings actually read. The "Skills Theory"—the idea that you can teach "Critical Thinking" without teaching "Facts"—had been conclusively debunked decades before the Common Core was written.

In 1988, researchers **Donna Recht** and **Lauren Leslie** conducted the definitive experiment on reading comprehension, now known as the **"Baseball Study."**

They took 64 junior high students and divided them into four quadrants based on two variables: **Reading Ability** (High vs. Low) and **Baseball Knowledge** (High vs. Low). They gave all the students a text describing a half-inning of a baseball game and tested their comprehension.

If the "Skills Hypothesis" were true, the "High Ability" readers—the students with the fast processors—should have outperformed the "Low Ability" readers, regardless of the topic. The results were an epistemological shock.

The "High Ability" readers who knew nothing about baseball **failed**. They could decode the words, but they could not construct the meaning. Conversely, the "Low Ability" readers who knew baseball—the "bad readers"—**outperformed** the "good readers" significantly (Recht & Leslie, 1988).

The study proved that "Comprehension" is not a transferable skill; it is a function of **Schema**. The "bad readers" succeeded because they possessed an internal map of the game (three strikes make an out; a bunt is a sacrifice). They could hang the new information on an existing hook. The "good readers" failed because their minds were **Empty Vessels**. They were trying to build a house without bricks.

By choosing to empty the curriculum of "Content" (History, Literature, Civics) to focus on "Skills" (Finding the Main Idea), the Common Core architects chose to ignore the biological reality of the human mind. They built a system designed to produce students who could read nothing, because they knew nothing.
