# Chapter 7: History Is Shrinking

> "You own nothing. You are licensing a temporary visual experience."
> — **Ubisoft Customer Support**, responding to the deletion of *The Crew* (2024)

> "The web is not a library. It is a commercial real estate project where the rent is due every month."
> — **Pew Research Center**, *Digital Decay* (2024)

### 7.1 The Handshake: The Webmaster's Witness (1998)

The "Gentleman’s Agreement" that held the internet together for thirty years was not a treaty signed in Geneva. It was a text file named `robots.txt`, and I remember exactly how fragile it felt when I first typed it.

In 1998, I was working as the webmaster for **Lippincott Williams & Wilkins (LWW)**, a division of Wolters Kluwer. My job was not to keep the web open; my job was to close it. I was tasked with building the company’s **first pay-per-article system**. We were erecting the high walls that would eventually lock up the world’s scientific knowledge, actively separating "Verified Knowledge" (The Hoard) from the chaos of the open internet.

But while I was hard-coding credit card gateways to keep humans out, I was simultaneously hand-editing a simple text file to let the machines in.

I remember staring at the command line, editing the file that controlled the behavior of the early web crawlers. The syntax was almost comically simple:

```text
User-agent: *
Disallow: /private/

```

I looked at it and thought: *Wait. This isn't a lock. This is a polite request.*

The irony was stark. I was spending thousands of dollars and man-hours on encryption and authentication to ensure that no human could read a PDF without paying $35. Yet, to stop a bot from taking the entire library, all I had was this text file. Technically, `robots.txt` does absolutely nothing to stop a crawler. It relies entirely on the honor of the visitor.

In 1998, that was enough. The crawlers from AltaVista, Yahoo, and the nascent Google respected the signage. We struck a delicate bargain: we allowed Google Scholar to peek over the wall to index our metadata (facilitating discovery/SEO), provided they didn't steal the asset. It was a handshake. They got the traffic; we kept the ownership.

But handshakes only work between people who have shame.

### 7.2 The Trespass

Fast forward to 2026. The "Gentleman’s Agreement" is dead. The companies currently scraping the web—OpenAI, Perplexity, Anthropic—do not view `robots.txt` as a rule; they view it as a suggestion they are wealthy enough to ignore.

They are not "crawling" to index the web for you; they are "consuming" the web to replace you. When Perplexity bypasses the `Disallow` tag on a news site, they are not acting like the search engines of 1998. They are acting like burglars who realized the door I built back in 1998 was never actually locked—it was just closed.

This is the **"Ingestion Flip."** For two decades, the implicit contract of the web was **Discovery for Access**. You let Google crawl your site because Google sent users to your site. The crawler was a delivery mechanism.

Today, the crawler is a **Digestion Mechanism**. The AI model ingests your content not to direct users to you, but to answer the user's question *instead* of you. They have broken the economic loop. They are using the "Polite Sign" I posted in 1998 as a doormat.

### 7.3 The Memory Hole: The Burning of the Yellow Pages

While the AI companies strip-mine the live web, the commercial web itself is rotting. In May 2024, the **Pew Research Center** published a forensic audit of the digital record titled *Digital Decay*. The results were a tombstone for the idea of the "Permanent Record." The study found that **38% of all webpages that existed in 2013 are gone**. They are not archived; they are 404.

To understand the violence of this erasure, we must measure the value of the victim. For a generation of users, **CNET** was not just a tech blog; it was the operating system of the culture.

Founded in 1994 by **Halsey Minor** and **Shelby Bonnie**, CNET (Computer Network) was the first entity to successfully industrialize the concept of "Tech News." It was the "Yellow Pages" of the silicon age. But its true power lay in a specific subdomain: `download.com`.

For fifteen years, this URL was the primary distribution node for the independent software industry. In 2005, `download.com` was serving **3.5 million downloads per day**. It was the "App Store" before the iPhone existed. It was the exclusive home of the tools that built the web: **WinZip**, **WS_FTP**, **Paint Shop Pro**, and **Notepad++**.

**The $1.8 Billion Jewel**
This utility created a "Community Lock-in" that was the envy of old media. CNET wasn't just broadcasting news; it was capturing the social graph of the early web through forums, user reviews, and walkthroughs.

In 2008, **CBS Corporation** recognized this power and acquired CNET for **$1.8 billion**. At the time, CBS CEO **Leslie Moonves** did not describe the purchase as a news acquisition. He described it as an infrastructure play. "CNET is one of the Top 10 Internet companies in the United States," Moonves told the press. "We are combining the premium content of CBS with the interactive engagement and **audience capture** of CNET".

Moonves understood that he wasn't buying headlines; he was buying the "Pre-Web 2.0" user base. He was buying the millions of people who came for a *Legend of Zelda* walkthrough on GameSpot and stayed for the forums.

**The Red Ventures Liquidation**
Twelve years later, that valuation had collapsed. In 2020, the private equity firm **Red Ventures** acquired CNET for just **$500 million**. They didn't see a "Jewel"; they saw a distressed asset full of "dead weight."

In the summer of 2023, the liquidation began. An internal memo from **Giselle Abramovich**, the VP of Content Strategy, authorized the deletion of thousands of articles. The forensic line was drawn at **2014**. Content created prior to this date that did not meet a specific "SEO Threshold" was designated for removal.

This wasn't just "old news." This was the primary source record of the internet. A 1996 review of Netscape Navigator is not "outdated consumer advice"; it is a historical record of the Browser Wars. A 2004 forum thread troubleshooting Windows XP is not "low-performing text"; it is the oral history of the digital infrastructure.

By deleting it, Red Ventures was effectively burning the library to save on the heating bill.

**The "Crawl Budget" Lie**
The justification for this arson was a technical superstition. The SEO consultants argued that this "Legacy Content" was consuming **"Crawl Budget"**—the finite attention Google’s indexers allocate to a site—without generating revenue.

**Danny Sullivan**, Google’s Search Liaison, publicly shouted into the void that this was not how the system worked. "Are you deleting content because you think Google doesn't like 'old' content? That's not a thing!" Sullivan tweeted. "Our guidance doesn't encourage this."

But the industry ignored the engineer and obeyed the algorithm.

**The Golden Escalator**
The tragedy is not just that we lost the files; it is what we left behind. The **2014** cut-off date is the punchline to a deadly serious joke.

By establishing 2014 as the "Event Horizon" of relevant knowledge, the SEO industry has ensured that the AI's memory of human history begins almost exactly at the moment **Donald Trump rode a golden escalator** to the ground floor of Trump Tower to announce his candidacy.

We have lobotomized the machine. We have surgically removed the context of the open, optimistic early web and trapped the AI in a "Presentist" reality that starts with the polarization, the screaming, and the fragmentation of the post-truth era. The machine doesn't just have Recency Bias; it has trauma. It has no reference point for a world before the noise.

### 7.4 The Patient Zero: The Haworth Liquidation

To understand the economics of this erasure, we must look at the moment the "Independent Archive" died. The "Patient Zero" of the Dependency Model was the acquisition of **The Haworth Press** by the conglomerate Taylor & Francis in 2007.

In Chapter 3, I described how Haworth’s physical books were fed to the guillotine. But the greater crime was what happened to its identity. Haworth Press, under Bill Cohen, was a bastion of niche, high-fidelity academic publishing. It had a specific editorial voice.

When it was absorbed into the **"Big Deal"**—the massive subscription bundles sold by the Big Five publishers—that editorial context was liquidated. The 190+ journals were not preserved as a coherent collection; they were stripped for parts. The metadata was standardized, the unique branding was dissolved, and the articles were dumped into the "Content Slurry" of the Taylor & Francis online platform.

This was the early proof of concept for the **Dependency Model**. By consolidating independent witnesses into a single corporate "Matmos," the industry proved that knowledge is only valuable when it can be aggregated. The "Gentleman’s Agreement" of independent stewardship was replaced by the "Service Level Agreement" of the monopoly.

### 7.5 The End of Ownership: The Ubisoft Precedent

If the Haworth acquisition proved they could liquidate the *publisher*, the case of *The Crew* proved they could liquidate the *product* in your hand.

In March 2024, the video game giant **Ubisoft** shut down the servers for *The Crew*, a racing game played by 12 million people. But they didn't just turn off the multiplayer mode. Because the game required a constant "handshake" with the server to verify the license, the shutdown rendered the physical discs and digital downloads completely inert.

Customers who had paid $60 for the game found that they owned nothing. They had not purchased a game; they had purchased a **"Revocable Access License."**

**Ross Scott**, a YouTuber who launched the "Stop Killing Games" campaign, identified this as a crossing of the Rubicon. "This is not about video games," Scott testified. "This is about the destruction of property rights.".

He is right. The logic applied to *The Crew* is the same logic applied to the digital library. In the "Dependency Model," you never own the book, the game, or the record. You are merely renting a window into the server. And as soon as the cost of electricity exceeds the value of your subscription, the landlord will brick the window.

The "Gentleman’s Agreement" of 1998 assumed that if you bought a copy, you owned it. The reality of 2026 is that you are only allowed to see it as long as the machine finds it profitable to show you.
