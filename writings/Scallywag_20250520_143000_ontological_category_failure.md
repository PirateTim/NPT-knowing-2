### 1. The 1980s: Nike, the Beatles, and Commercial Commodification
In 1987, Nike paid $500,000 to license the Beatles' 1968 track *"Revolution"* for a television commercial introducing air-cushioned running shoes (Capitol Records, Inc. v. Nike, Inc., 1987). For the first time, a original master recording of an iconic countercultural anthem—written amid global political upheavals—was repurposed to sell rubber footwear. The political urgency was severed, leaving behind a sterile aesthetic shell.

### 2. The 1990s: The Californian Ideology
In their foundational treatise, Richard Barbrook and Andy Cameron identified the fusion of 1960s West Coast countercultural rhetoric with hyper-capitalist free-market dogma (Barbrook & Cameron, 1996 [URL: https://www.mute.org/the-californian-ideology/]). The "Californian Ideology" promised that digital networks would inherently liberate humanity, while simultaneously privatizing public knowledge infrastructure. This ideological sleight-of-hand reframed corporate monopolies as benevolent countercultural pioneers.

### 3. The 2010s–2020s: Vector Embeddings and Ad-Tech Reductionism
With the rise of word embeddings (Mikolov et al., 2013 [URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/1301.3781]) and Transformer architectures (Vaswani et al., 2017 [URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.03762]), cultural artifacts were mathematically reduced to point clouds in high-dimensional vector spaces. In these spatial models, semantic similarity is calculated purely through token co-occurrence and spatial proximity. Historical provenance, irony, and tragedy are invisible to spatial coordinate geometry. A track about planetary doom is vectorially adjacent to "uplifting classic rock" if both share structural frequency signatures.

### 4. Present Day: Generative Slop and Automated Vandalism
We have reached the terminal stage: generative AI models trained on billions of ungrounded tokens produce endless streams of context-free content. Human curators, having abdicated critical evaluation, accept the algorithmic recommendation without reading the lyrics or understanding the text. The result is Meta selling "human connection" using a track celebrating the end of the world.
