My feed has recently contained several items about reading and thinking and the future of reading and thinking. So let's have a hard look at the people trying to shape the future of thinking.

Four very different dispatches landed on my desk this week, each offering a small, distinct window into how our culture is currently processing the written word. The first was a sweeping historical investigation by the Harvard historian Jill Lepore in Wired—an adapted excerpt from her forthcoming book, The Rise and Fall of the Artificial State—which carefully reconstructs the long, peculiar habit of computer scientists borrowing concepts, jargon, and moral justifications from the science fiction paperbacks of their youth. The second was a reflective commentary published on the Substack The Book Stop, which itself was an extended meditation on Matteo Wong’s reporting in The Atlantic regarding the quiet, documented collapse of deep reading across the American public. The third was a pedagogical field report in Reactor Mag by the educator Joanna Nelius-Free, recounting what happens inside high school and college classrooms when a teacher asks modern students to put away their automated homework assistants and actually wrestle with classic dystopian literature. And the fourth was a forensic survey from AI Tools Observer, cataloging the strange modern ritual whereby the chief executives of the world's most heavily capitalized artificial intelligence laboratories routinely publish multi-thousand-word manifestos promising that their computational clusters are on the verge of inaugurating an era of unprecedented human flourishing.

On the surface, these four dispatches seem to belong to entirely separate conversations. One is an academic excavation of literary history, one is a popular reflection on reading habits, one is an urgent dispatch from a high school classroom, and the last is a corporate discourse analysis. But when you place them side by side on the table and examine how their lines intersect, they reveal something far more coherent and disturbing. We are not simply living through a routine technological transition in software engineering or computational data retrieval. We are witnessing the direct collision between a fifty-year campaign of educational reform that systematically stripped the humanities of their critical core, and the sudden ascent of a small group of men who possess staggering global power, yet who suffer from a profound and specific intellectual disability: they can process every single word on a page, but they are entirely blind to human ideas.

To understand the scale of what is happening, we have to begin by clearing away a common and lazy misconception. The men leading this technological revolution—Elon Musk, Sam Altman, Mark Zuckerberg, and Jeff Bezos—are not bumbling comic figures, incompetent frauds, or cartoon villains. To treat them as clowns is to miss the entire reality of the world we live in. These men command balance sheets that exceed the gross domestic products of sovereign nations. They control the orbital launch manifests that determine what enters space, they own and route the deep-sea fiber optic cables that carry planetary communications, they negotiate dedicated multi-gigawatt power contracts with nuclear utility operators, and they direct the labor of hundreds of thousands of the most brilliant applied mathematicians and software engineers on Earth. Their physical machinery works with terrifying, ruthless efficiency. Their rocket boosters fall through the upper atmosphere and land backwards on autonomous ocean barges with millimeter precision. Their failure is not mechanical, and it is not a lack of logistical competence. Their catastrophic flaw is conceptual. They are exceptionally gifted technicians who are fundamentally incapable of recognizing, evaluating, or understanding a philosophical, moral, or political idea.

This blindness is not an aesthetic quirk or a simple matter of personal taste. It is not that tech executives fail to appreciate poetic cadence or dislike rhyming verse. The problem is structural: they consume literature the way a software compiler parses a stream of raw code. They read voraciously, but they read strictly for nouns, mechanical mechanisms, and gadgets. They inventory the spaceships, catalog the technological gizmos, track the sequence of functional actions, and treat the narrative as an engineering blueprint. What remains completely invisible to them is the underlying concept—the philosophical premise, the tragic irony, the political critique, or the ethical warning that gives the text its meaning. They seize the cool machine, and they throw away the moral reckoning.

Consider how this plays out in the foundational texts of computer science. In his standard university textbook on artificial intelligence, the computer scientist Stuart Russell turns to the ancient Greek myth of King Midas. For thousands of years, human civilization understood the story of Midas as a profound moral tragedy about the destructive nature of boundless greed and the catastrophic folly of desiring absolute material accumulation at the expense of life and family. Yet as the science fiction author Ted Chiang pointed out in a devastating critique, Russell examines the myth of Midas and diagnoses it not as a moral lesson about human avarice, but as a classic specification error—a mere prompt engineering bug where King Midas made an algorithmic mistake by forgetting to include an explicit exclusion clause in his wish that prevented his food, wine, and daughter from turning into gold. When you view the world entirely through this literalist lens, tragedy disappears, moral reckoning becomes a syntax error, and the deepest philosophical critiques of human nature are reduced to an issue of parameter tuning.

The exact same intellectual pattern appears when you look at Sam Altman, the chief executive of OpenAI. When discussing his philosophical worldview and his personal investments, Altman has repeatedly pointed to Dennis E. Taylor’s science fiction novel We Are Legion (We Are Bob). Taylor’s book is a dark, satirical space opera that begins when a software entrepreneur has his brain cryogenically frozen, only to wake up centuries later to discover that an authoritarian theocratic state has declared his frozen consciousness to be legal corporate property, stripping him of his human rights and forcing his digital mind into an automated space probe to serve as an expendable scout for the empire. It is an explicit, terrifying cautionary tale about the complete commodification of human consciousness and the loss of individual autonomy to state and corporate power. Yet Altman read this horrifying narrative and came away with a completely inverted takeaway: he was so enchanted by the literal technology of brain preservation that he invested millions of dollars into a biotechnology startup focused on cryogenically freezing human heads, happily treating a dystopian warning about digital slavery as an inspirational brochure for transhumanist estate planning.

Or take Elon Musk, who has spent more than two decades telling interviewers that his entire cosmic philosophy was shaped by reading Douglas Adams’ The Hitchhiker’s Guide to the Galaxy as a teenager in Pretoria. Musk has named SpaceX vessels after Adams’ starship Heart of Gold, had the phrase "DON'T PANIC" printed onto the dashboard of the sports car he launched into solar orbit, and frequently adopts the erratic, flamboyant public persona of Zaphod Beeblebrox, the galactic president who uses chaotic media spectacles to distract the public while private interests plunder the universe. But as Jill Lepore reminds us in her excavation of early computing culture, Douglas Adams was a committed, razor-sharp satirist who typed his anti-authoritarian masterpieces on an old manual typewriter adorned with an "END APARTHEID" sticker. Adams was not providing an investment prospectus for private planetary conquest or bespoke luxury terraforming; he was writing a savage parody of bureaucratic vanity, corporate fraud, and the supreme arrogance of technocrats who believe the cosmos exists to be carved up into private real estate. Musk seized on the gadgets, the spaceships, and the colorful galactic names, while remaining entirely oblivious to the fact that Douglas Adams had written an entire book mocking men exactly like him.

To understand how our ruling industrial class came to possess such immense technical skill while remaining entirely blind to human ideas, we have to look back at the quiet, systematic transformation of American and Western education over the last fifty years. This did not happen by accident. In the nineteenth century, reformers like Horace Mann envisioned the public schoolhouse as the democratic foundry of the sovereign citizen—an institution designed to equip ordinary human beings with the historical knowledge, rhetorical discipline, and philosophical grounding necessary to govern themselves in a self-governing republic. But throughout the twentieth century, that civic ideal was steadily dismantled and replaced by an industrial efficiency model, championed by administrators like Ellwood Cubberley, which viewed students not as sovereign citizens, but as raw economic inputs to be processed, standardized, and fitted into the machinery of corporate enterprise.

Over the past four decades, this utilitarian shift reached its absolute peak within elite engineering, applied mathematics, and computer science departments. In the race to produce high-velocity programmers and systems architects, universities systematically severed technical curricula from the humanities. Philosophy, rhetoric, intellectual history, and literature were pushed to the margins, treated as optional decorative electives rather than the essential intellectual armor required to evaluate power and human consequence. Students were trained to solve closed optimization problems, parse syntactical rules, and measure success strictly by throughput and computational speed. Even in primary and secondary education, reforms like the Common Core institutionalized this shift, prioritizing informational compliance over deep narrative interpretation. Architects of these educational standards, like Jason Zimba, openly admitted that reading benchmarks were calibrated not for scholars or critical thinkers, but for the basic functional requirements of entry-level corporate labor, while figures like David Coleman famously declared that the world does not care what students think or feel. The end result was a quiet, generational lobotomy: we systematically trained generations of brilliant technicians to believe that context is irrelevant, that language is merely a tool for functional execution, and that grappling with human ambiguity is an operational friction to be engineered away.

It is against this historical backdrop that the recent barrage of Silicon Valley manifestos becomes crystal clear. When leaders like Mark Zuckerberg, Marc Andreessen, Dario Amodei, and Sam Altman publish their sprawling essays on artificial intelligence, they are not engaging in genuine intellectual debate. They are deploying a carefully engineered fog of high-minded, frictionless abstractions—promising universal abundance, the elimination of disease, and the elevation of human potential—designed to project an aura of moral inevitability over a business model that relies on the massive, non-consensual harvesting of the human cultural commons, the extraction of gigawatts from regional energy grids, and the quiet liquidation of skilled human labor. And it is no coincidence that the technology they have built—the Large Language Model—operates on the exact same intellectual principles. Rooted in the statistical traditions of computational linguistics, the modern transformer does not possess an internal model of the physical world, a moral framework, or an understanding of cause, effect, and truth. It calculates the statistical probability of the next word in a sequence based on vast historical corpora. The technocrats have built a machine that mirrors their own reading habits: a system that is fluent, rapid, grammatically impeccable, and entirely blind to the ideas contained within the words it produces.

Nothing illustrates the devastating real-world consequence of this blindness more vividly than the scene that played out in the West Texas desert in October 2021. William Shatner, the ninety-year-old actor who had spent more than half a century portraying an imaginary starship captain exploring the cosmos, stepped out of Jeff Bezos’s New Shepard space capsule. Shatner was visibly shaken, weeping openly as he tried to put into words what he had just witnessed from the capsule window. He had expected to see the triumphant, infinite frontier of science fiction. Instead, he saw the terrifying, sudden darkness of the void, and beneath it, a breathtakingly thin, fragile blue ribbon of atmosphere wrapping around the living Earth—a fragile skin separating all known life from complete annihilation. It was a moment of profound, tragic human awe and ecological grief. But standing right beside him, Jeff Bezos was entirely oblivious to the idea Shatner was trying to express. Bezos did not look at the sky, and he did not listen to Shatner. Instead, he turned his back to the weeping actor, faced the waiting television cameras, and popped an oversized bottle of champagne, spraying it over the crowd in a loud, rehearsed spectacle of commercial triumph. It was the entire tragedy of our moment captured in a single frame: the human being mourning the vulnerability of the living world, while the oligarch pops corks to celebrate the machinery.

More than a century before that desert flight, in 1909, the English novelist E.M. Forster published a short story titled The Machine Stops. In Forster’s hauntingly prophetic world, humanity lives in subterranean isolation, sealed inside tiny domestic rooms where all physical and intellectual needs are mediated by an omnipotent, global computational network known simply as the Machine. The inhabitants do not touch each other, do not travel, and do not look at the sky. They spend their days consuming third-hand ideas, listening to lectures transmitted through glowing plates, and worshipping the Machine until they become completely incapable of surviving outside its mechanical embrace. In her classroom dispatch for Reactor Mag, Joanna Nelius-Free describes the sudden, electric shock of recognition that occurs when contemporary students are asked to sit down with Forster’s 1909 text. When stripped of their smartphones and guided through the slow, demanding labor of reading Forster’s actual prose, the students do not see an ancient relic of early science fiction. They see their own lives. They recognize that their increasing reliance on automated summaries, algorithmic feeds, and conversational AI is not the dawn of a revolutionary intellectual epoch, but the voluntary surrender to an old and documented trap of comfortable dependency.

That classroom realization is where our focus must remain. The great crisis of our time is not that machines are on the verge of developing human consciousness or launching an autonomous rebellion against their creators. The real crisis is that the physical and digital architecture of our world is being constructed and governed by men who were educated to think like machines—men whose staggering logistical competence is matched only by their total inability to comprehend the moral, political, and philosophical stakes of human culture. They have turned the written record of human thought into a raw training set, strip-mining centuries of struggle, literature, and philosophy into statistical weights, and they are now inviting the rest of us to adopt their own literalist blindness. The defense against this quiet enclosure does not require breaking machines or retreating into nostalgic despair. It requires something far more radical and demanding: the stubborn, disciplined refusal to outsource our own critical faculties. It means returning to the slow, friction-heavy, and un-automated labor of deep reading, demanding to know where an idea came from, understanding what it costs, and insisting on the human provenance of our thoughts. Because if we surrender the ability to understand ideas, we will find that we have built an astonishingly efficient, trillion-dollar machine—and trapped ourselves inside it.
