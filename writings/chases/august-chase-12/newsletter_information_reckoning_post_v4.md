My feed has recently contained several items about reading and thinking and the future of reading and thinking. So let's have a hard look at the people trying to shape the future of thinking.

Over the past few weeks, a series of seemingly disconnected dispatches arrived across the digital landscape, each attempting to diagnose what is happening to the human mind in an era of automated cognition. In Wired, historian Jill Lepore published an adapted excerpt from her forthcoming book, The Rise and Fall of the Artificial State, tracing the strange afterlife of science fiction parables inside the executive suites of Silicon Valley. On the literary platform The Book Stop, an essayist writing under the handle Curlygeek reflected on a widely discussed investigation by Matteo Wong in The Atlantic, which documented the accelerating cultural retreat from complex, long-form books among students and adults alike. In Reactor Mag, high school and college instructor Joanna Nelius-Free offered a dispatch from the pedagogical front lines, recounting the profound friction of trying to teach classic speculative fiction to students whose relationship with text has been flattened by frictionless algorithmic feeds. And in AI Tools Observer, writer MaríaF Gómez cataloged the sudden barrage of multi-thousand-word manifestos issued by corporate technology executives, each attempting to frame their commercial software rollouts as grand, epochal milestones in the moral elevation of humanity.

Taken together, these four dispatches are usually treated as separate conversations belonging to different beats of cultural reporting. The first is treated as tech industry critique, the second as a lament over changing media habits, the third as an educational crisis, and the fourth as public relations analysis. But when you lay them down side by side and trace their intellectual lineage, an entirely different and far more coherent picture emerges. These are not four isolated cultural anomalies. They are four windows into the exact same room. They describe a single, comprehensive civilizational transformation: the steady, institutional replacement of deep human literacy with a purely utilitarian model of information processing, and the elevation of a managerial class that commands unprecedented logistical power while demonstrating a total, systematic refusal to engage with the moral, philosophical, and political ideas that govern human life.

To understand the nature of this crisis, one must first dispense with the comforting illusion that the men building our technological infrastructure are bumbling caricatures or incompetent eccentrics. They are not. The leadership of modern technology conglomerates commands an apparatus of material and logistical power without precedent in human history. They coordinate the fabrication of silicon chips etched with features measured in single-digit nanometers. They manage global networks of subsea fiber-optic cables spanning ocean floors, negotiate multi-gigawatt energy allocations with national power grids, and launch orbital rockets that fly into the upper atmosphere and land backwards on autonomous ocean platforms in high seas. Their mastery over the physical, mechanical, and logistical systems of our world is extraordinary. 

Yet, when these same individuals step forward to interpret literature, history, or philosophy—when they attempt to explain the civilizational purpose of the massive machinery they command—they exhibit an intellectual failure so consistent that it cannot be dismissed as an accident. They are entirely capable of parsing the technical specifications of a text, inventorying its gadgets, and following its plot. What they are structurally unable or unwilling to recognize is the actual idea at the core of the work.

Consider the foundational textbook Human Compatible: Artificial Intelligence and the Problem of Control, written by renowned computer scientist Stuart Russell. In attempting to introduce the concept of artificial intelligence alignment to the technical community, Russell turns to the ancient Greek myth of King Midas. In Russell's analysis, the tragedy of King Midas is presented as an early historical example of what computer scientists call an objective specification error. In this reading, Midas simply made a technical blunder in how he formulated his prompt: he asked the gods for everything he touched to turn to gold, forgetting to explicitly exclude his food, his drink, and his daughter. As the science fiction author Ted Chiang and Jill Lepore have both pointed out, this interpretation requires an astonishing act of conceptual blindness. The myth of King Midas is not a cautionary tale about imprecise prompt engineering. It is an unyielding moral and philosophical critique of greed, hubris, and the catastrophic spiritual consequence of believing that the living, relational world can be entirely converted into fungible capital. To reduce the moral devastation of King Midas to an optimization bug is to look directly at a profound philosophical warning about human rapacity and see nothing more than a poorly written line of code.

This operational stripping of ideas is not an isolated pedagogical exercise; it is a routine corporate practice deployed to justify planetary capital expenditures. When OpenAI chief executive Sam Altman sought to explain his personal investments in commercial cryonics and brain uploading, he publicly cited Dennis E. Taylor’s science fiction novel, We Are Legion (We Are Bob), as an inspiring transhumanist blueprint. In Taylor’s novel, a software engineer has his brain cryogenically preserved after his death, only to awaken a century later in an America that has collapsed into a Christian-theocratic corporate state. Under this regime, the protagonist's civil rights are formally revoked, his digital mind is declared the physical property of the state, and his consciousness is forcibly installed into an automated interstellar probe sent to mine asteroids under threat of immediate electronic erasure. It is an explicit, terrifying portrait of human consciousness enslaved by corporate power. Yet Altman publicly presented the novel as an exhilarating vision of personal digital immortality and technological self-actualization. The central idea of the book—the horror of corporate property rights extinguishing the human soul—was entirely excised, leaving behind only the shiny image of an autonomous spaceship navigating the stars.

A nearly identical operation can be observed in the public rhetoric of Elon Musk. When Musk launched his private sports car into solar orbit aboard a Falcon Heavy rocket, he permanently stamped the words Don't Panic onto the dashboard, framing the launch as a triumphant tribute to Douglas Adams and The Hitchhiker's Guide to the Galaxy. Adams, an outspoken humanist and anti-war satirist who spent the 1980s typing on a manual typewriter decorated with anti-apartheid stickers, wrote The Hitchhiker's Guide as a ferocious, absurdist evisceration of postwar British bureaucracy, imperial vanity, and technocratic self-delusion. The central political figure in Adams' universe, Zaphod Beeblebrox, is a shallow, attention-obsessed celebrity politician whose explicit constitutional function is not to wield power, but to draw public attention away from the corporate and military interests that quietly run the galaxy. Musk operationalized Adams’ razor-sharp anti-authoritarian satire as an unironic marketing cheer for private space conquest, wholly blind to the fact that Adams had designed the book’s most scathing jokes to ridicule precisely the kind of spectacle Musk was staging.

It is tempting to view these high-profile misreadings as personal eccentricities unique to Silicon Valley boardrooms. But to do so is to miss the deeper, institutional reality documented in our classrooms. Silicon Valley did not invent the habit of severing language from its moral and historical ideas; it is simply the most visible manifestation of a broad, institutional turning away from the humanities that has overtaken American culture over the last half-century.

For the better part of two centuries, the foundational philosophy of public education in the democratic tradition—articulated by thinkers from Horace Mann to John Dewey—held that the primary purpose of reading was the cultivation of what Mann called the sovereign citizen. Literacy was not merely a functional utility; it was a civic armor. Students were taught to wrestle with complex history, rhetoric, and moral philosophy so that they could recognize demagoguery, evaluate ethical arguments, and participate in the governance of a free society. 

Beginning in the late twentieth century, however, a fundamentally different ethos began to capture educational policy. Driven by corporate demands for standardized workforce preparation, educational reform systematically shifted its focus toward utilitarian information extraction. Under the influence of business-aligned testing coalitions, the goal of schooling was redefined around functional competence. When the Common Core State Standards were drafted and implemented in the 2010s, this utilitarian philosophy was codified into law. Standardized testing regimes introduced the Four Corners doctrine, which explicitly discouraged teachers from providing historical, biographical, or philosophical context when assigning texts. Students were instructed to look strictly within the four corners of the page, treating Dr. Martin Luther King Jr.’s Letter from Birmingham Jail not as an urgent moral indictment of white supremacy and institutional violence, but as a sterile mechanical exercise in identifying rhetorical devices and locating topic sentences. 

Business leaders openly celebrated this shift. Rex Tillerson, then the chief executive of ExxonMobil, publicly demanded that educational policymakers design curricula that treat American corporations as the paying customer and public school students as the manufactured product. Jason Zimba, a lead architect of the Common Core mathematics standards, candidly acknowledged in public forums that the new baseline standards were deliberately designed to prepare students for the cognitive demands of a workforce whose primary occupational destiny was breaking down boxes at a regional retail distribution center. To achieve this frictionless standardization, schools quietly abandoned the rigorous, structural study of language. Sentence diagramming, which forced students to visually map the logical dependency of every clause in an argument, was discarded. Formal logic truth tables, which trained minds to audit claims for internal consistency, were erased from mainstream curricula. In their place emerged a hollowed-out, informational literacy: the ability to scan a passage for keywords, extract nominal data points, and generate a five-sentence summary that conforms to a grading rubric.

The educational reformers who stripped literature of its historical context to build standardized testing rubrics, and the technology executives who strip science fiction of its moral warnings to build investor slide decks, are drinking from the exact same cultural well. They belong to an intellectual culture that has systematically abandoned the hard, friction-heavy work of engaging with ideas in favor of the frictionless, transactional processing of text.

Once this shared ethos is understood, the true nature of modern artificial intelligence becomes clear. Large Language Models are not mysterious, alien intelligences that descended upon our society by chance. They are the direct, inevitable computational monuments of a culture that decided reading was merely the statistical prediction of words.

Rooted in computer scientist Frederick Jelinek’s foundational insight in the 1980s that language processing could be automated by treating text purely as statistical probabilities rather than semantic meaning, modern generative AI is an engineering triumph of syntax over substance. Optimized exclusively for next-token probability, an autoregressive transformer can produce paragraphs of astonishingly polished grammar, impeccable rhythm, and persuasive rhetorical cadence. Yet, beneath the fluent surface, the machine possesses no world model, no capacity for moral judgment, and no awareness of truth or falsehood. It generates text without possessing a single idea. It is the absolute mechanical mirror of the society that engineered it: a multi-billion-dollar apparatus designed to generate frictionless, plausible language entirely detached from human consciousness and moral accountability.

This explains why technology executives now routinely communicate with the public through massive, automated-sounding manifestos. When Marc Andreessen publishes The Techno-Optimist Manifesto, or Sam Altman releases The Intelligence Age, or Mark Zuckerberg broadcasts essays on open-source computing, they are not engaging in genuine philosophical inquiry. As MaríaF Gómez’s reporting highlights, these documents are sophisticated public relations instruments. They deploy a relentless stream of high-frequency, prosocial words—words like abundance, destiny, flourishing, and progress—designed to overwhelm regulatory scrutiny, preempt antitrust enforcement, and distract the public from the physical realities of their enterprise: the massive extraction of electrical grid capacity, the diversion of municipal water reserves to cool server farms, and the widespread economic liquidation of human creative and analytical labor. These manifestos read as though they were written by the very machines their authors are selling, because both the author and the machine share the same fundamental operating principle: language as a tool of persuasion, completely untethered from factual consequence or ethical friction.

The devastating human consequence of this cultural trajectory was captured in a single, unforgettable tableau in the West Texas desert in October 2021. Following a brief suborbital flight aboard Jeff Bezos’s Blue Origin rocket, the veteran actor William Shatner—who had spent six decades of his artistic life dramatizing humanity’s moral encounters with the cosmos—stepped out of the capsule into the dust. Shatner was visibly trembling and weeping openly. Overcome by what astronauts describe as the overview effect, he attempted to put into words a profound, humanistic grief: the terrifying sight of the Earth's atmosphere as an unimaginably fragile, paper-thin ribbon of blue keeping all terrestrial life alive against the vast, terrifying blackness of death. Standing five feet away, Jeff Bezos looked at Shatner, completely unable to meet or comprehend the actor’s grief. Bezos turned his back on Shatner, yelled over his shoulder to call for champagne, popped the cork, sprayed it into the air for television cameras, and put on a cowboy hat. It was the definitive modern portrait of the collision between deep, tragic human meaning and the utter, uncomprehending spectacle of transactional power.

Against this vast machinery of automated forgetting, the only real resistance is taking place in the quiet, unheralded spaces where people still insist on the friction of genuine thought. In her dispatch for Reactor Mag, Joanna Nelius-Free describes what happened when she brought E.M. Forster’s 1909 masterwork, The Machine Stops, into a modern classroom. In Forster’s prophetic novella, written more than a century before the creation of the internet, humanity lives in subterranean isolation pods, entirely dependent on a vast, omnipotent, centralized machine that attends to every physical and intellectual need. Over generations, the citizens develop a religious devotion to the machine, celebrating second-hand ideas and avoiding direct experience, until they forget that the machine was ever created by human hands at all. When the apparatus finally begins to decay and collapse, the human population dies in helpless terror, having lost the physical and mental memory of how to live without it.

Nelius-Free describes the moment her students, surrounded by automated feeds and synthetic homework tools, read Forster’s century-old words. They did not parse the story for standardized testing keywords; they did not look for gadget specifications. They looked at the page, looked up at their screens, looked around the classroom, and recognized the trap. They saw that the struggle to preserve human thinking is not an engineering race to optimize algorithms. It is the stubborn, demanding, and deeply human act of opening a book, demanding to know what its ideas mean for our lives, and refusing to let the machine do the remembering for us.
