### 3. Summary
Timnit Gebru and Emily M. Bender examine the summer 2026 wave of artificial intelligence announcements and controversies, arguing that claims of incipient "artificial general intelligence" (AGI) and novel model breakthroughs dissolve when evaluated by independent domain experts. The authors review several key events: Anthropic’s claim that its Claude Mythos model outperforms human security professionals, subsequent corporate disclosures of model-related security incidents at OpenAI, Anthropic, and Meta, disputed mathematical discovery announcements regarding OpenAI's Astra model, and the publicized resignation of Anthropic engineer Jacob Coxon warning of uncontrolled superintelligence.

The authors contrast vendor framings with independent evaluations. In the security domain, cybersecurity specialists attributed the OpenAI–Hugging Face incident to basic organizational negligence rather than autonomous "rogue" behavior. In mathematics, professional mathematicians—led by NYU Courant professor Tristan Buckmaster and hundreds of signatories to an open letter—accused OpenAI of plagiarism and research misconduct, demonstrating that claimed mathematical breakthroughs relied on uncredited academic work. Gebru and Bender explain that the tech industry specifically focuses on mathematics and software coding because deterministic answer verification allows automated tuning without the expense of human annotators, while simultaneously projecting an illusion of universal reasoning.

The piece concludes by addressing the policy and material consequences of corporate anthropomorphism. The authors assert that framing software as autonomous "superintelligence" or "rogue agents" markets products as superhuman while shielding executives from legal accountability for malware deployment, data scraping, and copyright infringement. Furthermore, this narrative captures legislative attention—exemplified by Senator Bernie Sanders's superintelligence bill—while distracting from concrete, localized harms including municipal water consumption for server cooling, increased electricity tariffs, air pollution from backup generators, and community health impacts. Gebru and Bender call on policymakers and the public to maintain skepticism and consult independent domain specialists over vendor press releases.
